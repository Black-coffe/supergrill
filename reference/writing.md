# Lens: writing — texts, articles, letters, scripts, documents

Goal: sharpen the text's THINKING before polishing its wording. A beautifully written wrong argument is worse than an ugly right one. Default level: `quick` for a short piece, `deep` for anything with claims that need evidence.

## Question tracks

1. **The one-sentence thesis** — what is this text arguing, announcing, or asking? If the user can't say it in one sentence, the text can't either. Grill until the sentence exists.
2. **Reader & change** — who exactly reads this, and what should be DIFFERENT in their head or behaviour after? "Inform" is not a change.
3. **The reader's resistance** — what does the reader currently believe that this text must overcome? Where will they stop reading, and why?
4. **Evidence audit** — go claim by claim: which claims carry the argument, and what does each stand on? Every adjective without a number, every "significant / growing / leading" gets challenged: what's the figure? Public figures are verified per `fact-check.md`.
5. **Structure as argument** — does the order of sections mirror the logic of persuasion, or the order the author thought of things? What's the strongest point — and why isn't it first (or is it)?
6. **The cut test** — for each section: if we delete it, what breaks? Nothing → delete.
7. **Voice & register** — who is speaking and how should they sound? Where does the current draft slip out of that voice?
8. **The counter-reader** — the most sceptical plausible reader writes a one-paragraph rebuttal. Can the text absorb it pre-emptively without bloating?

## Grounding

- If a draft is provided, quote it when challenging: point at THE sentence, not "the tone in general".
- If source materials are provided, verify claims in the draft against them; flag every unsourced number.
- If the text represents an organisation with a style system (the local overlay may name one), check candidate phrasing against those rules and say when a phrase would trip them.

## Super-level document checklist

- The draft (latest) and, if it exists, the previous version
- Source materials the draft relies on (data, reports, interviews)
- Style guide or examples of the target voice
- The brief or request that triggered the text (who asked for it and why)
- Feedback already received on earlier drafts

Suggested stages for `super`: (1) thesis, reader, change; (2) evidence audit, requires source materials; (3) structure, cut test, voice; (4) counter-reader and final editorial memo.

## Output

Brief = editorial memo: thesis (final wording), reader and intended change, claim–evidence table with gaps, structure verdict (keep / reorder / cut list), voice notes, the rebuttal and its answer.
Handoff offer: perform the rewrite from the brief (or generate the document via the organisation's document skill when the overlay names one).
